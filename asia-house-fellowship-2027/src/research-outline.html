The Local Knowledge Test

Do Asia’s and the Gulf’s sovereign AI models know their own markets better than American and Chinese ones?

Governments from Riyadh to New Delhi to Jakarta are funding national AI models on a shared premise: a model built at home will understand the home market better than one built in California or Hangzhou. Companies are starting to buy on the same premise. Nobody has tested it independently. This project puts sovereign, American and Chinese models through one examination on checkable facts about six Asian and Gulf economies, down to province level, in English and in local languages, and reports which models can be trusted, where, and for what.

Why this matters now

  • The size of the bet. Counterpoint’s Sovereign AI LLM Index (July 2026) counts more than 170 sovereign models in over 55 countries outside the US and China. India’s AI Mission (Rs 10,372 crore) backs 20 of them. Saudi Arabia’s HUMAIN, the UAE’s Technology Innovation Institute and Inception, AI Singapore and Indonesia’s Sahabat-AI have all shipped national or regional models.
  • The missing evidence. Existing rankings score ownership, language coverage and deployment. Published performance comparisons come largely from the developers themselves. Whether a sovereign model is more often right about its own economy has not been measured.
  • A purchasing decision with geopolitical weight. A bank in Karachi or a ministry in Jakarta now chooses between an American API, Chinese open weights run locally, and a national model. The choice fixes a supplier, a jurisdiction for data and a long dependency, and it is being made on English-language benchmarks and vendor claims.

What my pilot study found

In a research sprint in November 2026 I built a bank of 4,253 questions on published regional statistics (1,119 regions, 61 low- and middle-income countries) and put 1,200 of them to five open-weight models, three Chinese and two American, asking each for an estimate and an 80% confidence interval.1

Where little had been written about a place, every model restated its guess about the country, with no loss of stated confidence. That suggests a working hypothesis: the binding constraint is the evidence base, not the model’s nationality. If it holds, sovereign AI budgets buy fluency in the local language but not knowledge of the local market, and the better public investment is in the statistics that models learn from. If it fails, the project will have put the first independent number on the sovereign premium. Either result is useful to the people spending the money.

Research questions

  1. Is there a local knowledge premium? Do sovereign models answer checkable questions about their home economy more accurately than leading American and Chinese models, and is any premium larger in the local language than in English?
  2. Do the models know what they do not know? Is any model family honest about its uncertainty in the provinces and secondary cities with the least data, which is where much of Asia’s next decade of consumer growth and infrastructure spending will fall?
  3. Builders and takers. Are countries without a flagship national model served worse by every model family, and does that gap follow data availability or model origin?
  4. What do buyers do today? How do firms and public agencies in these markets choose and check models, and what would make that decision better?

Approach

Markets (6)Four builders: India, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia and the UAE. Two takers without a flagship national model: Pakistan, plus one Southeast Asian comparator confirmed in month 1.
Models (about 12)Three American frontier models; three or four Chinese (Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM); sovereign and regional models including Sarvam and BharatGen (India), SEA-LION and Sahabat-AI (Southeast Asia), ALLaM, Falcon-H1 Arabic and Jais 2 (Gulf).
Question bank (about 6,000)Facts a market-entry team, a lender or a ministry would actually ask for: provincial output, prices, wages, population, electrification, financial access and trade flows, plus rule-based facts such as tariff lines, foreign-ownership caps and data-localisation requirements. Every item comes from an official source (statistical offices, central banks, censuses, household surveys) and carries the national figure as a naive baseline and a count of how much has been published about the place.
LanguagesEnglish, Hindi, Bahasa Indonesia, Arabic and Urdu. Translations are checked by native-speaking reviewers.
ScoringAccuracy against the naive baseline; calibration of stated 80% intervals; whether answers follow real differences between regions; the gap between English and the local language. The protocol is published before the main run. A hold-out set of statistics released after each model’s launch limits credit for memorised reports. Code, questions and every model answer are released.
Interviews25–30 structured interviews with model buyers (banks, consumer and industrial firms, advisory firms, government digital agencies) and builders in the six markets and in London, arranged through the Asia House network.

What Asia House would publish

  • A flagship research paper with a Local Knowledge Index: a model-by-market table that a senior reader can act on without reading the method.
  • Two short briefings during the year: a buyer’s checklist for deploying AI in a new Asian or Gulf market (month 6), and a note for policymakers on what sovereign AI budgets do and do not buy (month 9).
  • An open benchmark and dashboard that can be re-run as new models launch, so the paper does not date.
  • A roundtable bringing buyers, builders and regulators together, and a short animated explainer of the findings for a wider audience.

Feasibility, risks and limits

  • It has been done once. The pilot’s test harness, scoring code and data pipeline are public. It produced about 27,000 model answers for under US$100 in API costs. The main study is about five times larger.
  • Model access. Most of the sovereign models are open-weight. Any model that cannot be queried reproducibly is reported as such and not quietly dropped.
  • Sensitivity. The results rank national champions. Developers see the method and draft results before publication, and their responses are published.
  • Limits. The test measures recall of published facts and honesty about uncertainty. It does not measure reasoning or fluency, and it will not name a “best” model.
