Personal statement · Asia House Fellowship 2027/2028
Karachi, Pakistan{{CONTACT}}

I come from Gilgit-Baltistan, the mountain region where the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor enters Pakistan. Large decisions are taken about places like it on very little evidence. Most of my working life has been spent on one side or the other of that problem: collecting evidence where there was none, checking numbers before they reached a decision-maker and, most recently, measuring how confidently AI models answer questions about places nobody has studied. I am applying for the Asia House Fellowship to turn that last piece of work into research that business leaders and policymakers across Asia and the Gulf can use.

I studied International Relations at the National Defence University in Islamabad, where my thesis examined what CPEC and tourism were doing to the ecology of my home region. I then took an MS in Business Analytics at the Karachi School of Business and Leadership because I wanted to test political-economy arguments with data, not only make them. My master’s thesis built a panel of 134 countries from 1990 to 2020 to ask whether trade openness reduces income inequality.

The result I still think about is a sign reversal. Across countries, more open economies look more equal (a correlation of −0.34). Within the same country over time, more openness goes with slightly more inequality (+0.025 Gini points for each percentage point of trade to GDP). The headline number and the truth for any one country pointed in opposite directions. I presented the paper at the GRID 2026 conference with my supervisor, Dr Sheraz Mustafa, and in November I presented a second paper, on who benefits from green digitalisation, at the Centre for Policy Dialogue’s Climate Week conference. The trade paper left me with a habit I now apply to AI: an average can be correct and still mislead you about the place you care about.

My jobs have been about making numbers trustworthy before anyone acts on them. At the Sindh Enterprise Incubation Center I designed the indicators, validation rules and dashboards for more than five entrepreneurship programmes and turned the results into reports for the Science and IT Department of the Government of Sindh. At The Dawood Foundation I now do the same for education and science-learning programmes. As a Research Fellow with Equitech Futures I carried out more than 40 interviews in Urdu and Balti on misinformation and institutional trust in Gilgit-Baltistan.

The clearest example of a project I drove on my own is the study behind my research proposal. In November 2026 I entered a three-day research sprint run by Apart Research with one question: do language models know less about places with little published research, and do they say so? No dataset existed, so I built one: 4,253 questions on regional statistics from Demographic and Health Surveys in 61 countries, each matched to its true value, to the national figure and to a count of published studies that name the region. Working alone, I wrote the test harness, ran five models under four conditions (about 27,000 model answers, for less than US$100), analysed the results and wrote them up as a working paper, with all code and data public.

Two things went wrong and I had to catch them myself. A pilot that put 100 questions into one prompt had to be thrown away because the answers influenced each other. Later, an output limit silently discarded most answers from the reasoning models until I noticed the gap and re-ran every missing question. My hypothesis also turned out to be only half right. I expected models to be most overconfident where nobody had looked. They were overconfident everywhere, and what thin evidence took away was the regional content of the answer. I reported it that way. I also tested my own proposed fix, which was to tell the model how thin the evidence is, against a placebo. It did no better than a made-up number, and I reported that too.

I have also built things with other people. I founded Young Shapers in Gilgit-Baltistan in 2019 and led it until 2022. It reached more than 600 young people, and its Act Clean Fellowship selected 80 fellows from over 300 applicants and supported 19 locally led environmental projects across eight districts. Mehfooz Internet, the digital-literacy initiative I ran from 2024 to 2025, was a finalist in the Rhodes Trust’s Kevin Xu Innovation Challenge.

Most of the debate about AI in Asia and the Gulf is about supply: chips, data centres and sovereign funds. I think the demand side is under-examined. A model is only worth its cost if it is right about the market in front of it, and for much of this region, below the level of the capital city, nobody has checked. That is the gap I want to work on, and there are three reasons Asia House is the place to do it.

The first reason is the audience. The people who decide which AI model a bank, a ministry or a manufacturer will depend on are senior business and policy leaders, and they are the readers Asia House writes for. A finding that stays in a technical preprint will not change a procurement decision in Riyadh, Jakarta or Karachi. An Asia House paper and a roundtable can.

The second is fit with work the Fellowship has already produced. Recent and current Fellows have worked on Gulf–ASEAN investment in AI infrastructure, AI adoption in Indonesia’s public sector, Saudi Arabia’s bid to become an AI and semiconductor hub, and the semiconductor supply chain in India, Malaysia and Vietnam. That work covers the chips, the data centres and the institutions. Mine asks the question that follows once those are built: do the models work for the markets they are meant to serve? I would like my results to be read alongside theirs.

The third is practical. A quarter of my project is interviews with the firms and agencies that buy and build these models. I cannot reach those people from Karachi on my own. Through Asia House’s network I can.

Twelve months with Asia House’s research team would also teach me to write for a far more senior readership than I have written for so far. Afterwards I intend to continue this work from Pakistan, as independent evaluation of the AI systems being sold into South Asia and the Gulf.
