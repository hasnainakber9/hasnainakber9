# 1. Mission and required outcome

Create a polished, emotionally engaging short film about a near-future
food system that works as one connected network: healthy soil and
resilient farms, useful science, human-supervised AI, sensible
logistics, affordable food, cultural choice, safe surplus
redistribution, and nutrients returned to the land. The audience should
feel this future is desirable and achievable—not magical, sterile or
perfect.

| **ONE-SENTENCE IDEA** The future of food is a loop, not a line: grow well, move food where it is needed, share safe surplus, and return unavoidable scraps to the soil. |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

## Deliverables

- Finished master MP4, around 80 seconds and always under 2 minutes.

- Editable Unity project/copy with organized scenes, camera setup,
  timeline, assets and scripts.

- 16:9 master, 1920×1080 or higher if practical, 24 fps, H.264 MP4,
  stereo 48 kHz audio.

- Optional 9:16 social cut made by reframing the existing edit, not by
  regenerating the film.

- Accurate captions (.srt) and a captioned version for silent autoplay.

- Thumbnail/poster, short social caption, asset/licence log, production
  note and submission checklist.

## Success test

The viewer enters through a small human moment, discovers a surprisingly
sophisticated food network, laughs once, understands the idea without a
lecture, and leaves with one memorable line they would share. Technology
is infrastructure that helps people, not a hero that replaces them.

## Judging criteria

# 3. Strategy to maximise our chances

No creative approach guarantees a win. Optimise directly for the
published criteria; do not assume visual complexity alone will win.

**Hook in 2–3 seconds —** Open on an imperfect but edible tomato about
to be discarded. A child calls it ugly; an adult answers with a small
joke.

**One governing idea —** Everything supports 'the food system is a loop,
not a line.' Do not create a random montage of futuristic gadgets.

**A real mini-story —** Follow the tomato/meal through growing,
forecasting, transport, preparation, sharing and nutrient return. Return
to the opening motif at the end.

**Plausible science —** Use near-term technologies: climate-resilient
crops, precision irrigation, soil monitoring, AI-assisted forecasting,
efficient cold chains, suitable controlled-environment growing,
fermentation, safe food rescue, composting and anaerobic digestion.

**Human agency —** forecasts warn and software coordinates; farmers,
workers, scientists, cooks and communities decide and act. Show a
neighbourhood answering a farm's call before a storm.

**Equity by design —** Show affordable access, dignified food
distribution, different cuisines, workers with agency and accessible
spaces. Never use people as a diversity checklist.

**A memorable finish —** End with 'GOOD FOOD. NO DEAD ENDS.' The story
should earn the line.

**Shareability —** One warm comic beat, one satisfying visual loop, a
strong thumbnail and a clear first frame. It should make sense without
knowing our team.

**Restraint —** Fewer convincing details beat ten unexplained
technologies.

# 4. Creative concept and world rules

## Working title

GOOD FOOD. NO DEAD ENDS.

## Logline

In a believable near-future region, one imperfect tomato leads us
through a food system combining resilient agriculture, farmer-informed
AI, appropriate city growing, food science, efficient distribution,
affordable markets, a shared meal and nutrient return.

## Visual world

- Hyperrealistic cinematic 3D: natural skin, tactile food, leaf veins,
  soil, stainless steel, crates, condensation and kitchen steam.

- Near-future, approximately 2040s in feel, but show no year on screen.

- A coherent region: mixed farms/plains, a city greenhouse, a modest
  food-science facility, a logistics hub, neighbourhood market and
  community kitchen.

- Warm lived-in spaces; practical engineering; diverse and recognisable
  food cultures; no luxury-only utopia.

- Use a consistent colour grade, lighting direction, wardrobe, character
  proportions and camera language across Unity and AI-generated footage.

## Plausible system elements

- Soil and moisture sensors, targeted irrigation, resilient crop
  varieties, rotations, hedgerows and pollinator habitat.

- AI-assisted harvest/demand forecasts corrected by farmers and local
  coordinators.

- Greenhouses or vertical farms for suitable perishable crops—not all
  staple crops.

- Precision fermentation or microbial ingredients as one option among
  many, not a magical replacement for all food.

- Electric delivery vehicles, efficient cold storage and reusable
  crates.

- Safe, unserved surplus redirected promptly to food-rescue partners;
  plate scraps and inedible material handled separately.

- Inedible scraps processed through composting or suitable anaerobic
  digestion/nutrient recovery. Never imply instant conversion to edible
  food.

- Farmers and food workers remain skilled, paid participants; automation
  should assist rather than erase their livelihoods.

## Avoid

- Cyberpunk neon, hologram overload, humanoid robot chefs, magic
  replicators, instant crop growth or impossible physics.

- A single tower farm supposedly feeding the world; food appearing from
  nowhere; AI deciding who deserves food.

- Unsafe donation of plate scraps; unsupported statistics; claims that
  hunger or waste is completely solved.

- Generic corporate sustainability imagery, tokenistic representation,
  sterile all-white cities or people posed at camera.

# 5. Character bible and dialogue rules

**ASHA — farmer and co-op lead, 40s  
**Practical, observant, dry humour. Knows soil and weather better than a
dashboard. A decision-maker, not a rustic prop.

**MAYA — food scientist, early 30s  
**Curious, energetic, slightly nerdy. Her facility feels like a real
pilot plant. She treats fermentation as food craft and science, not
magic.

**LUIS — regional food-logistics coordinator, 30s–40s  
**Calm under pressure, connects producers, kitchens and delivery routes.
Uses screens as tools but speaks to people.

**NOOR — curious child, around 10–12  
**Quick, funny and perceptive. Asks simple questions that expose how the
system works. Never speaks like an adult futurist.

## Absolute dialogue rules

- NO NARRATOR. NO VOICEOVER. No explanatory presenter and no disembodied
  AI voice.

- All speech is diegetic: the character is physically in the scene and
  speaking to another person.

- Use short, natural lines, pauses, interruptions, small jokes,
  reactions and slight overlaps.

- Characters never explain what the audience can already see. They must
  not recite the contest theme.

- Use separate dialogue tracks when possible; keep voice identities
  consistent. Never imitate a real person's voice without permission.

- Use profiles, over-the-shoulder angles, cutaways and wide shots to
  avoid uncanny lip-sync in every shot.

# 7. Unity + AI Video Maker workflow

## Inspect the actual tools first

- Inspect the existing Unity project, version, render pipeline,
  packages, assets and scene structure before editing. Make a
  backup/copy first.

- Check whether Unity's official agent plugin/MCP workflow is installed.
  It is distinct from Unity Recorder and distinct from the AI Video
  Maker app/plugin.

- This brief assumes 'video maker plugin' means the connected AI Video
  Maker app. Verify its exact name, availability, supported references,
  duration, audio features, model options, pricing and usage rights. If
  'Build Video Maker' refers to another package, inspect that exact
  package before using it.

- Do not pretend a missing tool is available. If the plugin is
  unavailable, proceed with the available Unity workflow and report the
  missing dependency.

- Do not purchase credits, start subscriptions, upload private material
  to unapproved services, post publicly or submit the entry without the
  user's approval.

## Use each tool for its strength

- Unity: consistent 3D world, recurring characters,
  farm/greenhouse/lab/hub/kitchen environments, camera moves, match cuts
  and final assembly.

- Unity Timeline/Cinemachine or existing equivalents: use if available;
  avoid unnecessary dependencies or destabilising upgrades.

- Unity Recorder: capture rendered scenes/video from the Unity Editor;
  confirm installed version and export settings.

- AI Video Maker: selectively generate high-value macro food, crop,
  landscape or other shots; use approved references for continuity; do
  not generate filler.

- Post-production editor: colour match, dialogue cleanup, sound design,
  captions, title card, reframing and final export.

- Hybrid rule: generated footage must match Unity's lens language,
  palette, wardrobe, lighting and geography. Replace visibly
  inconsistent shots rather than hiding them.

## Execution sequence

1.  Back up the project; never overwrite or delete the original.

2.  Create a reusable asset library: tomato, produce crates, crop rows,
    soil/irrigation elements, greenhouse modules, fermentation vessels,
    electric delivery van, food-hub shelves, kitchen table and
    compost/organics bins.

3.  Block all 10 shots with proxy assets and rough camera moves. Review
    the full animatic before polishing.

4.  Lock character appearance, clothing, proportions, voice identity and
    screen direction.

5.  Use low-resolution previews first. Render final quality only after
    timing, camera and dialogue are approved.

6.  Mix dialogue, ambience, effects and music on separate tracks. Keep
    dialogue spatially tied to the scene.

7.  Export a preview, watch it full-speed and on a phone-sized screen,
    then fix only high-impact defects.

8.  Render the master and confirm it plays end-to-end with correct
    aspect ratio, frame rate, picture and sound.

## Credit and cost discipline

- Use existing assets before generating new ones. Approve reference
  stills before expensive animation.

- One initial generation per planned AI shot. Review the whole edit
  before rerolling.

- Check live cost before every paid generation. AI Video Maker and
  Higgsfield credits may be different systems; never assume they are
  interchangeable.

- No purchases or top-ups without user approval. Stop if existing
  balance is insufficient.

- If Higgsfield is used, carry forward the prior constraint: 200 credits
  maximum total, with a preflight estimate for every generation.

# 9. Cinematography, edit and sound

## Camera language

- Use 24–35 mm equivalent for environments, 50 mm for character
  interactions and macro for food textures.

- Use tracking, short dolly moves, motivated pans, gentle crane reveals
  and rack focus. Every move must have a physical reason.

- Use tomato-red match cuts between fruit, sun, crates, sauce and table.
  Start sound from the next environment just before the visual cut.

- Avoid constant drone shots, excessive slow motion, unmotivated
  360-degree spins and AI morph transitions.

- Hold on faces and actions long enough for the audience to read
  behaviour.

## Sound

- No narration or voiceover. Dialogue is always in-scene.

- Use separate dialogue, ambience, effects and music tracks; keep voices
  consistent across shots.

- Ambience: wind in crops, irrigation, crate handling, cold-room hum,
  electric delivery, market chatter, chopping, pans, kitchen clatter and
  laughter.

- Music: contemporary cinematic electronic-acoustic score, gentle pulse,
  organic percussion, subtle strings and warm synths. No lyrics, generic
  corporate piano or oversized trailer brass.

- Let dialogue and ambience lead at times. Lower music before the final
  exchange; let the title land cleanly.

- Mix dialogue for phone speakers; avoid over-compression.

## Edit rhythm

- 0–6s: hook and humour.

- 6–30s: farm, human-guided forecast and city growing.

- 30–47s: food science and logistics.

- 47–66s: market and shared meal.

- 66–74s: nutrient loop.

- 74–80s: return to the tomato, final joke, title.
