# Confident Where Nobody Looked: Do AI Chatbots Know Less About Women in Under-Researched Regions, and Do They Say So?

**Name and affiliation:** Syed Hasnain Akber, Young Shapers (youth-led climate and civic initiative, Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral), Pakistan

## Introduction
Programme staff, journalists and students now ask AI chatbots for local statistics. For places that little research covers, often where needs are greatest, a chatbot may give a wrong number with false confidence, and that number can steer funding and attention. This study tested whether AI models know less about women in under-researched regions, whether they say so when they are unsure, and whether telling them how much research exists helps. It informs SDG 17 (data and emerging technologies), SDG 5 and SDG 10.

## Methodology
From Demographic and Health Surveys, 4,253 questions were built on child marriage, home births, anaemia, partner violence and menstrual hygiene in 1,119 regions of 61 countries, 16 of them in Asia and the Pacific. Each question has the published regional figure, the national figure and a count of published studies naming the region. Five open-weight AI models answered 1,200 questions, each with a best estimate and an 80% confidence range, then again with the true study count, a wrong count and a general caution. The study was run in an Apart Research sprint in November 2026.

## Findings, Outcomes and Implications
The models were overconfident: the true figure fell inside their 80% range only 16% to 51% of the time. None beat simply quoting the national figure. Where fewer than ten studies exist, answers barely reflected how a region differs from its country. Telling a model the real study count helped no more than a wrong count. Surveys put adequate menstrual hygiene at 91% on average; the models said 48% to 65%. In the 16 Asia-Pacific countries the pattern held. Agencies should take subnational figures from survey reports, and AI tools must be tested where data on women is thinnest.
