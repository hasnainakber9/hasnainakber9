## Shortlist

### 1. Drought Intelligence Explorer: a 45-year district-level dataset of ten drought indicators for India (preprint + software)
A. Deshmukh · EarthArXiv · 11 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.31223/x5f52x · open access (CC BY)
- **What's new:** monthly drought indicators for all 724 Indian districts, Jan 1981 to Dec 2025, built by a scripted Google Earth Engine pipeline from CHIRPS, ERA5-Land and MODIS NDVI. The ten indicators are SPI and SPEI at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, a root-zone soil-moisture anomaly, and the Vegetation Condition Index (VCI). It comes with an open browser platform.
  - SPI and SPEI are standardised rainfall and rainfall-minus-evaporation indices: how unusual a period is in standard deviations.
- **Why it matters:** a district × month panel is exactly the structure your fixed-effects work needs. The same pipeline can run on Pakistan's districts and be joined to crop and livelihood data.
- **Reproducible?** ✅ Code v1.0.0 is on Zenodo/GitHub (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24113364, CC BY). It needs an Earth Engine account, which you have. It's a single-author preprint and not peer-reviewed yet.
- **Verdict:** FULL READ

### 2. CSIRO UNSEEN submission: 2022 Pakistan floods, seasonal and short timescales (datasets)
D. Irving, A. Stellema, J. Risbey · Zenodo · 12 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24150823 (Jul–Aug total) and https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24150570 (Rx5day) · open (CC BY)
- **What's new:** return periods for Pakistan's 2022 July–August rainfall and for Rx5day (the wettest 5-day total of the year). They come from the UNSEEN method applied to decadal forecasts from 9 DCPP models, as CSIRO's entry to the WCRP Common Event Attribution and Assessment case study.
  - UNSEEN pools many forecast-ensemble members into thousands of plausible years, so it can estimate how rare an extreme is when a 40-year record is too short to tell.
- **Why it matters:** the 2022 floods are the reference event for climate displacement in Pakistan. A defensible "1-in-N-year" figure strengthens any displacement or livelihood paper and the APFSD framing.
- **Reproducible?** ✅ Outputs and configuration are open. The underlying DCPP model data is very large and comes through ESGF.
- **Verdict:** FULL READ (method)

### 3. Evaluation of gridded data sets for representing precipitation event properties across 20 U.S. Gulf Coast locations (paper)
S. Kaiser, E. Ahmadisharaf, A. AghaKouchak et al. · *Geophysical Research Letters* · 10 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.1029/2026GL125547 · open access (CC BY-NC-ND)
- **What's new:** 128,141 rain events (2015–2024) were used to test eight products. Four use gauges (AORC, MRMS, MSWEP, Stage IV) and four don't (PERSIANN, IMERG, ERA5, MERRA-2).
  - Event volume is captured better than intensity or duration, because too-low intensity and too-long duration cancel out.
  - PERSIANN was the best gauge-free product for intensity.
  - Light, on-and-off rain is poorly captured by most products.
- **Why it matters:** a product can get rain totals right while getting the event shape wrong. Check intensity and duration before using IMERG or ERA5 for floods or crop stress in GB, where gauges are scarce.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 All products are public; I didn't check whether the analysis code is released.
- **Verdict:** FULL READ

### 4. Tracing vegetation stress from space: isoprene emissions capture the spatiotemporal footprint of El Niño (paper, late pick)
H. Liu, I. C. Prentice, C. Morfopoulos · *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science* · 20 Aug 2026 · https://doi.org/10.1038/s41612-026-01518-1 · open access (CC BY)
- **What's new:** satellite-derived isoprene emissions, a gas plants release under heat and water stress, rose by up to about 30% across tropical ecosystems during the 2015–16 El Niño. They followed the event's west-to-east progression. Leaf area index and solar-induced fluorescence (SIF) showed weaker, patchier responses.
- **Why it matters:** this is a faster stress signal than greenness, which is useful while this El Niño peaks. It covers tropical forests, though, not South Asian croplands.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 The isoprene product is satellite-derived; I didn't check code or data release.
- **Verdict:** QUICK SCAN

### 5. Precipitation and surface snow in CARRA2: a first pan-Arctic assessment (preprint)
ESS Open Archive · 10 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.22541/essoar.15011926/v1 · open access
- **What's new:** CARRA2, ERA5, ERA5-Land and IMERG V07 were checked against ground stations for 2000–2025.
  - Cold-season precipitation is too high in CARRA2 (+7.1%), ERA5 (+13.9%) and ERA5-Land (+13.8%), and slightly low in IMERG V07 (−3.1%).
  - For snow water equivalent (SWE, the water stored in the snowpack), CARRA2 is −16.7% and ERA5 is −8.7%, while ERA5-Land is **+37.1%**.
- **Why it matters:** this is the Arctic, not the Karakoram. But ERA5-Land's large snow overestimate is a warning for our snow-to-greenness work: use MODIS snow cover (as Mountain Watch does) rather than ERA5-Land SWE.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 Reanalyses are public; station networks vary.
- **Verdict:** QUICK SCAN

### 6. Glacier changes 1990–2025 and climate variability in the Tirich Mir region, Hindu Kush, Pakistan (preprint)
S. Ullah, S. Abbas, S. Khan et al. · Research Square · 11 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11445767/v1 · open access (CC BY)
- **What's new:** five glaciers in the Tirich Mir massif (Chitral) shrank from 64.23 to 52.81 km², a 17.8% loss. Area comes from Landsat NDSI at five dates, and the climate trends from NASA POWER.
- **Why it matters:** it's in Chitral. But the method is basic (snapshots of a snow index plus coarse climate data). Treat the numbers as indicative and cross-check against the Randolph Glacier Inventory or GLIMS before citing.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 The data is public; no code is listed.
- **Verdict:** QUICK SCAN

### 7. Replication package: groundwater depletion, drought and agricultural production, global gridded and country panels (dataset + code)
Q. Zhang, Z. Yang, X. Ai · Zenodo · 11 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24142260 · open (CC BY)
- **What's new:** a global 0.5° cropland panel for 2003–2024 combining MODIS greenness, ERA5-Land drought indices, GRACE/GRACE-FO groundwater trends and irrigation type. It adds FAOSTAT country panels of cereal output and food inflation. One script reproduces every published number.
- **Why it matters:** a ready-made panel-econometrics template built on satellite data, in the style you'd use for an Indus paper. I couldn't identify the parent paper yet.
- **Reproducible?** ✅ Data and code are included.
- **Verdict:** BOOKMARK

## Methods note
**ENSO is the same number for every district in a given year**, so a two-way fixed-effects model (district + year) absorbs it completely: you can't estimate "the effect of El Niño" with year fixed effects in. Two ways round this:
- interact ONI with a district characteristic (rainfed share, elevation, irrigation access), which identifies differential exposure;
- or drop year effects and use district trends, but then any other nationwide shock in an El Niño year (prices, policy) contaminates the estimate.

Cluster standard errors by district. As a robustness check, use Conley standard errors, which allow neighbouring districts' errors to be correlated, because weather is spatially correlated.

**Starter code** (`climate-digest/starter/2026-11-13_pakistan_district_rain_panel.py`, compiles; anomaly and ONI logic tested on sample data):
```python
import io, sys, requests, pandas as pd, xarray as xr
sys.path.insert(0, "climate-digest/tools"); import ee_auth
ee = ee_auth.init()
dist = ee.FeatureCollection("WM/geoLab/geoBoundaries/600/ADM2").filter(ee.Filter.eq("shapeGroup", "PAK"))
chirps = ee.ImageCollection("UCSB-CHG/CHIRPS/PENTAD").select("precipitation")
rows = []
for y in range(2001, 2026):
    months = [f"{y}-{m:02d}-01" for m in range(1, 13)]
    img = ee.Image.cat([chirps.filterDate(d, ee.Date(d).advance(1, "month")).sum().rename(d[:7]) for d in months])
    for f in img.reduceRegions(dist, ee.Reducer.mean(), scale=5000).getInfo()["features"]:
        rows += [{"district": f["properties"]["shapeName"], "month": d[:7], "rain_mm": f["properties"].get(d[:7])} for d in months]
df = pd.DataFrame(rows).dropna()
da = df.set_index(["district", "month"]).rain_mm.to_xarray()
da = da.assign_coords(time=("month", pd.to_datetime(da.month.values))).swap_dims(month="time").drop_vars("month")
clim = da.groupby("time.month")
z = ((clim - clim.mean("time")).groupby("time.month") / da.groupby("time.month").std("time")).drop_vars("month")
panel = xr.Dataset({"rain_mm": da, "rain_z": z}).to_dataframe().reset_index()
oni = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(requests.get("https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/data/indices/oni.ascii.txt").text), sep=r"\s+")
oni.columns = ["seas", "year", "total", "oni"]
oni["mon"] = oni.seas.map({s: i + 1 for i, s in enumerate("DJF JFM FMA MAM AMJ MJJ JJA JAS ASO SON OND NDJ".split())})
panel["year"], panel["mon"] = panel.time.dt.year, panel.time.dt.month
panel = panel.merge(oni[["year", "mon", "oni"]], on=["year", "mon"], how="left")
panel.to_csv("pak_district_rain_panel.csv", index=False)
```
