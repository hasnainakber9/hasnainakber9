## Shortlist

### 1. Environmental flows in a bypassed mountain river: satellite evidence from the shutdowns of the Neelum–Jhelum hydropower project (dataset + code)
Akram et al. (RUDN University; University of Agriculture, Faisalabad) · Zenodo · 23 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24465646 · open (CC BY 4.0, code MIT)
- **What's new:** the plant's shutdowns (Jul 2022–Jul 2023, and from May 2024) act as a switch. On the 34 km reach below the diversion, Sentinel-2 sees **9.5% less water when the plant runs** (95% CI 5.0–15.8%, Apr–Oct); Landsat gives 20.6%, and a regression gives −11.0%. Generation data come from NEPRA and flows from GloFAS v4.
- **Why it matters:** a Pakistani-administered HKH river, an Earth Engine pipeline and an on/off design you can read as a difference-in-differences.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 Earth Engine scripts plus a Python reproduction script. The README cites a shadow-corrected step-3 script that is missing from the zip. The paper is only submitted (River Research and Applications).
- **Verdict:** FULL READ

### 2. How many images does it take? Detection limits for channel-network change in the Landsat record of the Indus Delta (preprint)
Zaidi, Nisar (independent researchers) · EarthArXiv · 24 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.31223/x51f9m · open (CC BY 4.0), not peer reviewed
- **What's new:** an apparent 54% fall in channel-loop density (1990–2024) does not survive. Date-to-date variation is large (coefficient of variation 0.52–0.71 across 448 scenes), so detecting a 20% change with 80% power needs 118–185 clear dates per period. Much of the drift lines up with the switch from Landsat 5 to Landsat 8.
- **Why it matters:** Indus region, and the same logic as a sample-size calculation in econometrics. It applies to any trend from 26 MODIS winters.
- **Reproducible?** ✅ GitHub YMZaidi/indus-delta-detection-limits and Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.24449753 (MIT); imagery from Microsoft Planetary Computer, no login.
- **Verdict:** FULL READ

### 3. Extreme heat and labor-market reallocation: evidence from Iran (working paper)
Amanzadeh, Aghaei (Sharif University of Technology) · SSRN · 24 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7726658 · free to read, not peer reviewed
- **What's new:** one-year job transitions in Iran's Labor Force Survey, linked to daily county weather, with county, province-by-year and season fixed effects. Each 10 extra days at 35 °C or hotter raise exits from farming by 0.66 percentage points, and 0.57 points of that goes into unemployment or inactivity, not into industry or services.
- **Why it matters:** the design ports straight to Pakistan's Labour Force Survey with ERA5-Land heat days. It is the livelihoods step before migration.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 no code statement; the weather source isn't named on the landing page.
- **Verdict:** FULL READ

### 4. Climate shocks and entrepreneurial engagement: evidence from rural Southeast Asia (paper)
Lück, Revilla Diez · *Journal of Development Studies* · 20 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.1080/00220388.2026.2756230 · open access (CC BY-NC-ND)
- **What's new:** a two-way fixed-effects panel of rural households in Thailand and Vietnam, 2007–2022. Droughts push households into easy-entry trading businesses that don't last; tropical storms reduce entry and raise exit. The abstract gives no effect sizes.
- **Why it matters:** a clean template for "shock → livelihood switch" with a household panel; the same question fits BISP or PSLM data in Pakistan.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 TVSEP data on application; I couldn't see the climate products or a code statement.
- **Verdict:** QUICK SCAN

### 5. HYDRA: a global atlas of hydroclimate variability linked to ocean-atmosphere climate modes (preprint + dataset)
Samara, Seager, Smerdon (Columbia, Lamont-Doherty) · Research Square, under review at Scientific Data · 24 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11322731/v1 · open (CC BY 4.0)
- **What's new:** regression and composite maps of rain, evaporation and soil moisture against 10 climate indices, including ENSO and the IOD (1902–2024 for ocean modes), for all four seasons, from HadISST, CRU TS, ERA5, GLEAM and GPCP.

### 6. Accuracy rankings of gridded precipitation products do not transfer to engineering design: Kosovo (code + results)
Krasniqi, Rapuca, Hajdari (University of Prishtina) · Zenodo · 26 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24569489 · open (code MIT, results CC BY 4.0)
- **What's new:** ten products scored against 31 independent gauges, 2002–2021. Median daily KGE (a combined skill score; 1 is perfect): MSWEP without gauges 0.59, IMERG V07 0.50, ERA5-Land 0.43, CHIRPS v2 0.33. But the accuracy ranking barely predicts which product works best for drought indices (rank correlation 0.20) or extremes (0.06).
- **Why it matters:** "best product" depends on the use. Rank products for the task you have, not by a single skill score.
- **Reproducible?** 🟡 code and outputs open; the gauge data are not. Some return periods are implausible (billions of years), which looks like extreme-value extrapolation. The paper is "in review" at HESS.
- **Verdict:** QUICK SCAN

### 7. Intraseasonal rainfall characteristics of South Africa's Free State maize belt and their association with ENSO (paper)
Manatsa, Mahasa (Bindura University; University of the Free State) · *International Journal of Climatology* · 25 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.70697 · likely paywalled
- **What's new:** with CHIRPS (45 seasons) and IMERG V07B, El Niño seasons have fewer rain days and less rain from multi-day spells; spells of 3+ days deliver 82–88% of wet-spell rain.
- **Why it matters:** a template for asking how El Niño changes the *timing* of rain, not just the total, which matters more for crops. The authors say the effect weakens once the three strongest El Niños are dropped, and that it cannot be separated from the IOD.
- **Reproducible?** ✅ code and derived series on GitHub (dmanatsa-cloud).
- **Verdict:** QUICK SCAN

### 8. Vegetation-cooling thresholds and machine-learning prediction of land-surface temperature in irrigated drylands (paper)
Safdar et al. (Xi'an Univ. of Science and Technology; University of Agriculture, Faisalabad; and others) · *Remote Sensing* (MDPI) · 25 Nov 2026 · https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18234061 · open (CC BY)

## Methods note
**Before you trust a trend or a "record", ask how much data it would take to see one.** Item 2 shows a 54% Landsat decline that the data cannot actually detect. Item 6 shows that the most accurate rain product can be the wrong one for drought work. The Rwanda maize deposit in "Skipped" finds its "climate" signal is dwarfed by two level shifts that may come from FAOSTAT revisions. Three habits help:
- estimate the noise first (variation between dates, years or gauges), then ask what change you could detect;
- look for breaks that line up with a sensor switch or a data revision before reading them as real;
- test predictions out of sample against a plain baseline, as in this week's research target.

## Next research target
**Does this record snow start tell us anything about spring?** Gilgit-Baltistan's snow cover since 1 Oct is the highest in 26 winters. The useful question is whether that carries into March–April snow and summer water.
- **First result (already run):** no. Over 2001–2026, snow cover from 1 Oct to mid-November is uncorrelated with Mar–Apr snow cover (r = 0.01 for the region; −0.07 to 0.19 by district). In leave-one-winter-out tests it forecasts spring worse than the plain average in every district. Only **February** cover carries into spring (r = 0.47). For May–June cover, the early start does a little better (r = 0.29).
- **Data:** MODIS MOD10A2 snow cover (already in `projects/mountain-watch/data/`, through Earth Engine); DJF ONI from NOAA CPC; next, ERA5-Land snow water equivalent and snowfall (ARCO-ERA5, no login) and GloFAS v4 river flow for the Indus at Besham.
- **Method:** forecast skill against climatology with leave-one-winter-out tests. Then swap the target from snow *cover*, which saturates at 80–90% in spring (standard deviation only 2.2 points), to snow *water* or May–June flow, which vary more.
- **First 3 steps:**
  1. Run the starter; add May–June cover as a second target.
  2. Pull ERA5-Land Oct–Feb snowfall and Apr snow water equivalent by district; test whether snowfall predicts spring where cover does not.
  3. Add DJF ONI and the El Niño years only, and report the interval honestly with 26 winters.
- **Output:** a short Mountain Watch note ("Don't read November as a forecast") and a line in the AOGS2027 abstract (due 15 Jan).
- **Risks:** 26 winters is a small sample; MODIS misses snow under cloud and forest; ERA5-Land snow is often unreliable in the Karakoram.

**Starter code** (`climate-digest/starter/2026-11-27_early_snow_predicts_spring.py`). It compiles, and I ran it on the repo's data; the numbers above come from it.
```python
import io, requests, numpy as np, pandas as pd
D = "climate-digest/projects/mountain-watch/data/"
early = pd.read_csv(D + "snow_to_date.csv")[["water_year", "unit", "scf_to_date"]]
m = pd.read_csv(D + "snow_monthly.csv"); m["year"] = m.date.str[:4].astype(int)
spring = m[m.date.str[5:7].isin(["03", "04"])].groupby(["year", "unit"]).scf.mean().rename("scf_marapr").reset_index()
df = early.merge(spring, left_on=["water_year", "unit"], right_on=["year", "unit"]).dropna()
oni = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(requests.get("https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/data/indices/oni.ascii.txt", timeout=60).text), sep=r"\s+")
oni.columns = ["seas", "year", "total", "oni"]
df = df.merge(oni[oni.seas == "DJF"][["year", "oni"]], on="year", how="left")

def loo(sub, cols):                                    # leave-one-winter-out: RMSE of the model vs climatology
    err, clim = [], []
    for y in sub.year:
        tr, te = sub[sub.year != y], sub[sub.year == y]
        X = np.c_[np.ones(len(tr)), tr[cols]]; b = np.linalg.lstsq(X, tr.scf_marapr, rcond=None)[0]
        err.append((np.r_[1, te[cols].values[0]] @ b - te.scf_marapr.values[0]) ** 2)
        clim.append((tr.scf_marapr.mean() - te.scf_marapr.values[0]) ** 2)
    return np.sqrt(np.mean(err)), np.sqrt(np.mean(clim))

rows = []
for u, sub in df.groupby("unit"):
    r = sub[["scf_to_date", "scf_marapr"]].corr().iloc[0, 1]
    e1, c = loo(sub, ["scf_to_date"]); e2, _ = loo(sub, ["scf_to_date", "oni"])
    rows.append({"unit": u, "n": len(sub), "r_early_spring": r, "rmse_clim": c, "rmse_early": e1, "rmse_early_oni": e2})
print(pd.DataFrame(rows).round(2).sort_values("r_early_spring", ascending=False).to_string(index=False))
```
